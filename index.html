Bayda — Privacy Policy

Last updated: 21 September 2026

Bayda is a training, running and nutrition tracker for iOS. This policy describes exactly what it stores, where, and for how long. It is written against the app's actual database schema rather than from a template — every category below corresponds to a real table.

Controller: David Bayard (Baydalabs), Belgium.
Contact: bayard.david@hotmail.com

What Bayda stores

Health data (special category)

Bayda stores weight, body fat percentage, food and drink you log, and your training — exercises, sets, weights, repetitions, durations and session times. It also stores what you tell it so that it can estimate and track your targets:

  • your height, date of birth and sex, when you enter them or fill them from Apple Health while setting up a calorie estimate, or enter them in Profile › Body. Every calorie formula needs them, and they are saved to your account so the estimate can be worked out again later. Each of them can be left out;
  • how often you currently exercise (sessions a week) and how active your day is outside training;
  • your goal weight, which way you are heading (lose, maintain or gain), how fast, and when you set it;
  • your weekly activity goal and what it counts — strength workouts, runs, walks and rides, or both — and your daily step and weekly distance goals, if you set them;
  • your calorie and macro targets, and how each was set: estimated by Bayda, typed by you, or no target at all because you chose to log food only;
  • the history of your targets and weekly goals, with the dates they applied, so a day from last month is compared with the target you had then rather than the one you have now. The history is written by the database itself whenever a target or goal changes; the app can read it but cannot edit it;
  • the days in your food diary you mark as complete (or as not complete), so a day with nothing logged is not mistaken for a day you ate nothing.

Under the GDPR this is special-category data concerning health (Article 9). The lawful basis is your explicit consent (Article 9(2)(a)), given by creating an account and entering it. You can withdraw that consent at any time by deleting your account, which erases the data (see Deleting your account).

Your food diary, weight and body fat, height, date of birth, sex, calorie and macro targets, goals, the history of your targets and goals, the days you mark complete, and your choice of Home cards are never shared with anyone. There is no setting that shares them. They are readable only by your own account, enforced in the database itself and not merely in the app.

Your activities are different. Workouts, runs, walks and rides — including sessions you bring in from Apple Health — are activity posts. Who can see them follows Profile › Sharing, which is Anyone on Bayda unless you choose otherwise; see Training you choose to share. Someone you tag on a session can see that session even if it is set to Only me; see Tagging someone you trained with. GPS routes have their own, separate setting, which is public unless you change it — and a map is never shown more widely than the activity it belongs to.

Account and profile

Your account identifier, and the username, display name, profile picture and the short line about what you are working towards that you choose to show on your profile, plus what you use Bayda for and which summaries your Home screen shows. A username is required, because it is how other people find you — your email address is never searchable and is never shown to another user.

Training you choose to share

Bayda has a social feed. You can search for other accounts, follow them, and see the workouts of the people you follow; they can see yours on the same terms.

Who can see your workouts is Profile › Sharing › Who can see my workouts, and it defaults to Anyone on Bayda. The three values are Only me (nobody but you), Followers (the accounts that follow you), and Anyone on Bayda (any signed-in Bayda account). The setting is read at the moment somebody tries to look, not stored onto each workout — so changing it applies to everything you have already logged as well as everything new, in both directions. Tightening it hides your existing workouts immediately.

You can also set a single workout to Followers or Only me. That setting can only make one workout more private than your account already is; it can never make it less. On an account set to Followers, a workout left on Default is not published to everyone.

Anyone on Bayda can follow you, and there is no approval step: following is a one-tap action on both sides, and unfollowing is the same tap again. Bayda has no private accounts and no follow requests. If you do not want your training seen, the visibility setting above is the control — Only me hides everything you have logged from everyone, followers included. Your followers and the accounts you follow are visible to you.

Follows, likes and comments are stored, because storing them is what makes them work. A like or a comment carries your account and is visible to everyone who can see the workout it is on. Deleting a comment removes it. You can block an account, which stops it following you, tagging you, finding you in search and seeing your workouts; the accounts you have blocked are stored, and only you can see them.

Your email address is never searchable and is never shown to another user. Search matches the beginning of a username or of any word in a display name, from two characters, and shows at most 20 accounts at a time. You appear in it only while you leave Findable by username switched on, and never to an account you have blocked or that has blocked you, or while an account is suspended. An account that does not exist, one that has switched discoverability off, and one that has blocked you all look the same — simply absent — so search cannot tell anyone why a particular account is missing. Searches are rate-limited, so the directory cannot be quickly walked through. We should be clear that a search of this kind can be used, slowly, to list many of the accounts that have left discoverability on; if you would rather not be listed, switch it off. Tagging a training partner still needs their exact username.

Suggested athletes. Bayda can suggest accounts that are followed by people you follow, ranked by how many of them do. The most a suggestion can reveal about that is a count, never which of the people you follow follow them, because who someone else follows is not yours to see. Accounts that have switched off Findable by username, and accounts on either side of a block, are never suggested. If you dismiss a suggestion that choice is stored, and it is visible only to you; the person you dismissed is never told.

Tagging someone you trained with

You can tag another Bayda account on a session you recorded — the person you ran or lifted with. Two separate things happen, and the difference between them is deliberate.

On your own card, immediately. The tag is part of your post, so their name appears on it as soon as you add it, to everyone who can already see that workout under the rules above. This needs no permission from them, in the same way that writing a name in a caption does not.

On their profile, only if they accept. They are notified, and until they say yes the session does not appear on their profile and their name is not shown publicly on your card either — only you, in that workout's own screen, can see a tag nobody has answered yet. They can accept, decline, or withdraw an acceptance later. Declining is not announced to you; the tag simply stops appearing.

A tag lets the tagged person read that session, from the moment they are tagged, so they can see what they are being asked about — even if the workout would otherwise be outside what they are allowed to see, including a session set to Only me. Declining takes that access away again. The GPS route is never included: being tagged on a run does not give anyone the route it followed, because a route usually starts and ends at somebody's home.

Only the account owner can tag on their own session, and only the tagged person can answer. Nobody can add a name to a workout that is not theirs, and nobody can answer on someone else's behalf. Blocking an account, in either direction, prevents tags between you and ends any that already exist.

Bayda does not compare your location history against other people's to guess who you trained with. Some apps do this. Bayda does not do it, and does not run any matching of that kind in the background. Tagging is something you do by hand, or not at all.

Location, while you record a run or a walk

Bayda records your location only while a run or a walk is recording (a ride counts as a run here), and only if you allow it. It asks for “while using the app” permission — never “always” — so it cannot locate you when you are not recording. The background permission it does use keeps a recording you started running with the screen off, and stops when you stop.

What is kept is the path itself: a list of the positions your phone reported, stored with the workout, plus the distance, pace and elevation worked out from it. Positions your phone reported inaccurately are discarded rather than stored, and when the signal drops out Bayda never invents a path across the gap — the route you and anyone else see is only the ground your phone actually reported.

Steps and motion, if you allow it

Bayda can read your step count from your phone's motion sensor, which is a separate permission from location and is optional. It asks the first time you record a walk, or the first time a run loses its GPS signal — not when you sign up.

It is used for two things. It shows how many steps a run or a walk took, and when GPS drops out it asks the step counter how far you went during exactly that stretch, so the distance is not missing the tunnel you ran through. Any distance worked out this way is labelled on the summary as an estimate, with its size in kilometres, and it never changes your route or your pace — both of those stay measured from GPS alone. How much of the distance was estimated is saved with the run, so the label travels with it: the feed card and the run’s own page say the same thing the summary did, rather than showing an estimate as though it had been measured. Steps are read on your phone; the step count itself is not uploaded.

If you refuse this permission, or your phone has no step counter, Bayda behaves exactly as it did before: a stretch with no GPS is reported as not counted, and the steps figure shows a dash rather than a zero.

GPS routes are governed by a separate setting, because a route usually begins and ends at your home. It is Anyone on Bayda unless you change it, so by default the map of a run is shown to whoever can see the run. A new route takes the more private of that setting and the run’s own visibility, so if your routes are set to private a public run still posts without a map. The setting is stamped onto each route when it is saved, so loosening it later never retroactively publishes a route you already recorded. Tightening it works the other way, at once: setting your routes to private, or a run to Only me, hides maps you have already shared, including from anyone tagged on the run. One exception, made once: on 16 September 2026, while Bayda was a small invited test group, route maps recorded before that date were opened to match the visibility of the run they belong to, when routes stopped being private by default. A run or an account set to Only me kept its map private.

What other people see of a route, and what they do not

When someone else can see one of your routes, they are not shown the route you recorded. Before it is ever published, Bayda’s database draws a random distance between 150 and 450 metres, separately for the start and the finish of each run, and removes every part of the route within that distance of where you started or where you finished — wherever in the run it passes them, so laps of your own block or a pass back by your door are hidden too. If that leaves the route in more than one piece, only the longest piece is shown; pieces are never joined up across the part that was removed, and where a gap in the recording would draw a straight line through it, the route is broken there too. The map on a feed card is that line, and it is labelled to say the ends are hidden. Only you can see your own complete route.

This happens on the server, not in the app, and the full route is stored in a table that no other account can read under any setting. We do it this way deliberately: a trim performed by the app would still leave the complete path sitting in data another person’s phone could fetch.

A run that stays entirely inside its own hidden zones publishes no map at all rather than a fragment. The run itself still appears, with its distance and time.

We should be straight with you about the limits of this. Hiding the ends of a route raises the effort needed to work out where you live; it does not make it impossible. Published research on other apps that do the same thing has shown that with enough activities from the same starting point, and with the total distance of each one known, a determined person can narrow down a hidden location. Choosing a random distance each time is what defeats the simplest version of that attack, and it is not a guarantee. It also cannot hide a place the route never recorded: if the first position was fixed only once you were already away from your door — a watch still finding GPS, which some imported activities show — the hidden area is centred there, not on your home. If you would rather share nothing, set your route visibility to private — your runs will still post, without maps.

If you turn off Precise Location, Bayda will not record a run at all. The positions it would get are accurate to about a kilometre, which is not a route, and recording one anyway would mean storing a path you did not take.

Apple Health

Bayda asks for its Apple Health access all at once, in one Apple sheet — the first time you use anything that needs Health, or when Bayda notices it has never been given part of it. Asking for everything together is so that nothing ends up half connected: a walk from your watch arriving without its map, for example. Each item in Apple's sheet is still a separate permission you can switch off, there or later in your iPhone's Health settings, and Bayda works without any of them. Being allowed to read something is not the same as reading it: each item below is read only when you use what it is for.

What it reads.

  • Your step count, for the steps figure, to show what you have actually walked instead of guessing.
  • Your weight and body fat percentage, when you use the weigh-in screens, so you do not have to re-type what your scale already recorded.
  • When you set up a calorie estimate, your height, date of birth, sex and latest weight, which every calorie formula needs. Bayda reads them when you choose to fill them from Apple Health, and fills in only the fields you have left empty.
  • For your own runs, walks and rides, the heart rate your watch recorded during each one and the energy it measured, so its page can show your heart rate and what you actually burned rather than Bayda's estimate of it. They are read on your phone when you open the activity, and never uploaded; nobody else who opens it sees them.
  • Only if you ask it to, a list of the runs, walks and rides other apps and watches recorded in the last 90 days, so you can choose which of them to bring in — and, for a session that is brought in, the route your watch stored with it, so its map, its kilometre splits and its climb come across rather than only a distance and a time. Unless you switch on automatic import, described below, Bayda never brings in an activity you have not picked.
  • For a run, walk or ride you have already brought in that arrived without its route — a watch sends the map separately, sometimes minutes after the workout — Bayda looks again for that route when you open the app or the activity, for up to two weeks, and adds it when Health has it. The map then appears on that activity under the same route setting as any other, without you doing anything further.

There is one thing it reads without being asked. When it saves a run, walk or ride to Health, Bayda first looks at the workouts Health already holds around that same time, to see whether your watch recorded the same session — so that it does not add a second copy of it. That check reads only when those workouts started, how long they lasted, and what type they were. It is limited to the session you have just finished, it happens only when saving to Health is on, and nothing it finds is stored or uploaded.

What it writes, and only if you turn it on. Saving activities to Apple Health is a choice you make for your account. It is offered once, on the summary of the first activity you save, and you can change it at any time in Profile › Preferences › Save activities to Apple Health. Nothing is written to Health unless that switch is on and iOS allows it; either way, your activity is saved in Bayda. Turning it on never copies your earlier sessions across — it applies to activities you save from then on.

With it on, Bayda writes the workouts, runs, walks and rides you finish, with their distance, when you paused, and an estimate of the energy burned, so they count towards your Activity rings. The pauses are written so that the workout in Health is as long as the run rather than as long as the afternoon. For an outdoor activity it also writes the route it recorded, so the map appears in Health beside the workout. That copy is the complete route, not the shortened one other people see — it is on your own device, in your own Health store.

It does not write the same thing twice, and this is deliberate. If your watch already measured the energy for a session, Bayda writes no energy figure of its own — your Activity rings are a total across every app, so a second figure for the same minutes would count that session twice. And if Health already holds an overlapping workout of the same type from another source — a Watch recording the run you also tracked in Bayda — Bayda writes nothing at all for it: no workout, no route, no energy. One session should be one entry in your day, and when there are two the one Bayda skips is its own.

What leaves your device, and what does not. Your steps and heart rate are read and used on your device only — they are never uploaded to Bayda's servers. Three things are different, and all three are things you ask for: height, date of birth and sex you fill from Health (or type) while setting up a calorie estimate are saved to your account, as described under Health data; a weight you choose to save becomes a weigh-in on your account; and a session brought in from Health is copied to your account as a workout of your own, with its date, duration, distance and the route your watch recorded. That copy is then treated exactly like a session Bayda recorded itself, including the visibility rules above; the route is stored with its own separate privacy setting, which defaults to public, exactly as a route Bayda recorded does. Nothing is copied until you tap Add on that particular session — unless you have switched on automatic import, in which case sessions Apple records after that point are copied as they happen.

What a saved run, walk or ride keeps. Alongside its kilometre splits, each activity keeps a timeline of elapsed time, distance covered and moving time, so its pace chart and its splits can be drawn again later. It holds no locations, and anyone who can see the activity can read it, exactly as they can read its splits. Its altitude profile is kept separately and only you can read it, because it begins where you began. When Bayda estimates the calories an activity used, that estimate is saved to your account together with the weight and the published method it was worked out from, and only you can read it. Heart rate is still never uploaded: the activity’s page reads it from Apple Health on your phone each time you open it, and shows it to nobody else.

Bringing sessions in automatically. There is a switch, off unless you turn it on, under Profile › Preferences › Add Apple Watch workouts automatically. With it on, iOS wakes Bayda in the background whenever Apple records a run, walk or ride, and that session is added to your account — with its route — without you opening the app. Three things follow, and all three are worth saying plainly:

  • An automatically added session is shared exactly as one you record in Bayda is. It takes your default visibility — Anyone on Bayda unless you have changed it — so the session can reach the feed before you have picked your phone up. If you would rather that not happen, either leave this switch off or set Who can see my workouts to Only me.
  • It applies only from the moment you turn it on. Sessions from before that are never swept up; they stay in the list, and are added only if you pick them yourself.
  • Only what Apple itself recorded is taken automatically. A workout another fitness app wrote into Health is offered in the list instead, so a single session recorded by two apps is not added to your account twice.

Turning the switch off stops the background wake-ups as well as the importing.

You can revoke any of it at any time in iOS Settings → Privacy & Security → Health.

Photos on a workout

If you add a photo to a workout or a run, it is uploaded to Bayda's storage and shown to whoever can see that workout — the same set of people its privacy setting already governs, and no one else. A session set to Only me keeps its photo to you (and to anyone you tagged on it); a Followers session's photo is visible to followers.

Before this, a photo you added was stored only on the phone that took it, so nobody else ever saw one. That has changed, and it is worth saying plainly: a photo on a workout is shared content, governed by that workout's visibility. Deleting the workout deletes the photo with it.

Notifications

If you enable them, Bayda sends you reminders you set yourself — to weigh in, to log a meal, to train. These are scheduled on your device and their content never leaves it.

Bayda also stores your device’s push token so that the server can notify you when somebody follows you, likes or comments on one of your workouts, tags you on a session, or accepts a tag you added. Those notifications are also kept in your account’s notification list. The notification’s content is generated on the server at the moment it is sent; the request that triggers the send carries only an identifier and a timestamp, never your name and never the text of a comment.

Reports

If you report content, Bayda stores the report, what it referred to, and who sent it, so it can be reviewed.

What Bayda does NOT do

  • No advertising, and no advertising identifiers.
  • No third-party analytics or tracking SDKs.
  • Your data is never sold, rented, or shared with advertisers or data brokers.
  • No email/password accounts — sign-in is Apple or Google only, so Bayda never handles a password.

Where it is stored

Bayda uses Supabase (database, authentication, file storage and server functions) hosted in the European Union (eu-west-1, Ireland). Supabase acts as a processor.

Push notifications are delivered through Apple Push Notification service. Sign-in is handled by Apple and, if you choose it, Google; Bayda receives only the identifier and, on first sign-in with Apple, the name you allow it to share.

Open Food Facts. When you search for a food or scan a barcode, that search term or barcode is sent to Open Food Facts, a public food database, so it can be looked up. Bayda sends no account, no name, no device identifier, and no record of what you actually logged — Open Food Facts never learns whether you ate the thing you searched for. As with any request to any website, they do receive your IP address and the app's name, and they may log those.

Apple Maps. Drawing a route on a map means your device asks Apple for the map tiles that cover it. Apple therefore sees the area a map is being drawn for, whether the route is yours or somebody else's. That is Apple's own service under their privacy policy, and it is how any map on iOS works.

GitHub, for the update check. To tell you when a newer build is available, the app downloads a small public file from GitHub that holds the latest build number. The request carries nothing about you or your account, but as with any website GitHub receives your IP address and may log it.

How long it is kept

Your data is kept for as long as your account exists. When you delete your account it is erased immediately — see below. Search rate-limit records hold only your account identifier and the time of each search; any older than two hours are deleted whenever a search is made.

Your rights

Under the GDPR you may access, correct, export, restrict, object to, or erase your data. Two of these are built into the app and need no request:

  • Export — Profile → Your data → Export my data. Produces a complete JSON file of everything held about you, including your targets and settings, the history of your targets and goals, and the days you marked complete.
  • Erasure — Profile → Your data → Delete my account.

For anything else, or to complain, write to bayard.david@hotmail.com. You also have the right to lodge a complaint with the Belgian Data Protection Authority (gegevensbeschermingsautoriteit.be).

Deleting your account

Deletion removes your profile, workouts, sets, routes (and the shortened copies other people could see), routines, custom exercises, food log, weight history, recipes, targets and settings (including height, date of birth, sex and goals), the history of your targets and goals, the days you marked complete, notifications and push tokens, tags you added or were asked about, comments, likes, follows, blocks, dismissed suggestions, and the reports you sent. A report somebody else made about your content is kept so it can still be reviewed, but no longer records whose content it was.

Stored files are deleted too, not just the records pointing at them — your profile picture and any workout and exercise photos are removed from storage before any row is deleted, and the deletion is refused if any file survives. Bayda keeps only a one-way hash of the deleted account identifier and the date, as evidence the erasure was carried out; that hash cannot be used to identify you.

Children

Bayda is not intended for anyone under 16 and should not be used by them.
